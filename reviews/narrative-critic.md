# Narrative Critic — Round 1

## 1. The setting's three hooks are set up and then dropped: the Ring, the insider resistance, and the "hint of fantasy" — MAJOR (close to BLOCKING)

**Problem.** The game is named after the Ring, and the setting section makes three distinct promises. After the setup, the structure pays off none of them.

**Evidence.**
- *The Ring.* "The Ring is the connection between Marendel's system and the wider network of inhabited space... Severing it isolates the Directorate garrison and changes the conditions of the conflict" (lines 428). Severing it happens in Act 1 (**Sever the Ring**, line 344), the fourth operation of the campaign. After that the doc says only "The campaign then follows the local struggle" (430). It never says what "changes the conditions" means. Does the garrison get desperate or turn brutal? Is it cut off from relief? Is Marendel's population now cut off too? The titular object disappears for three of four acts, and the final act (432) ends without mentioning it.
- *The insider resistance.* "Nova's resistance grows from the Directorate's own logistics organization. Its leaders built the network while outwardly serving the occupation" (422). "A small force knows how the system works" (424). This is the most distinctive story idea in the document, but no mission, briefing, choice, or reward uses it. Operators are "highly trained resistance personnel" (216), not former logisticians. Side missions are generated (326). Briefings serve "the immediate situation, the job, and the equipment" (436). Nothing shows insider knowledge, divided loyalties, or the cost of having served the occupiers. The only trace is navy-and-blue uniform color (448).
- *"A hint of fantasy"* (420). It never shows up again in setting, art direction (444–452), or mission list.

**Why it matters.** As written, the narrative is a generic "resistance raids occupiers" frame. The specific premise is in the setting paragraph only, and the campaign structure doesn't use it.

## 2. The player's identity contradicts itself across sections — MAJOR

**Problem.** The doc gives three incompatible answers to "who is the player?"

**Evidence.**
- "The player becomes one of its skilled operators" (422): the player is an operator inside the fiction.
- "The player commands a standard squad of 4 operators drawn from a growing resistance roster" (29), and the War Table/Barracks framing (366–372): the player is a commander above the roster.
- "The opening escape introduces a single prisoner" (214), "an unarmed prisoner crossing a dangerous space" (511): the player starts as a captive, not a resistance operator.

There are also omissions. The doc doesn't say who the prisoner is, why a member of a still-covert resistance (the campaign "begins as that resistance starts acting openly," 422) is in Directorate custody, or whether the prisoner joins the roster and can be permanently lost like any operator (258, 308). If the player "is" an operator, permadeath of that operator has no defined story meaning. If the player is the commander, line 422 is wrong.

**Why it matters.** This decides who speaks in briefings, who the player is loyal to, and what losing the opening character means. It has to be settled before any dialogue is written, especially since the Voiceover Agent pipeline (535, 581) needs fixed speaker identities.

## 3. No authored characters on either side; the antagonist has no face or motive — MAJOR

**Problem.** The campaign builds to "defeat occupation command" / **Their Last Order** (347). But the document names no people anywhere: no Nova leader, no handler, no Directorate commander, no rescued medical team or field commander as characters. The Directorate's reason for occupying Marendel is never given.

**Evidence.**
- The Directorate is described only by force and look: "numbers, equipment, and entrenched positions" (424), "uniform and immaculate," gold for officers (450). Its enemy roster is by kit role (268–274). "Mission-specific commanders" (264) and "an occupation commander" (310) are unnamed mechanical priority targets.
- "Bring Them Home or Chain of Command — Restore a medical team or recover a field commander, opening the corresponding support kit" (360). The rescued people pay off as a kit unlock, not as characters.
- "Dialogue and directed shots give important moments a point of view" (436). But the doc never says whose point of view, and the voiceover contract (581) requires a "speaker identity" the design doesn't supply.

**Why it matters.** "Their Last Order" can't land as a climax against an opponent the player never met. The resistance's larger story ("the larger resistance benefits from the squad's operations," 349) has nobody in it to react to the player's victories.

## 4. The "bring your operators home" promise is undercut by how loss and failure are framed — MAJOR

**Problem.** The tagline (7) and pillar ("Familiar operators become the people the player wants to bring home," 66) make loss the emotional center. But the authored story never acknowledges loss. And the failure rules, as written, make a total wipe the less costly outcome in story terms.

**Evidence.**
- Operator stories are left to the player entirely: "Operator identities remain open to the player's interpretation" (440). Recognition after death is a memorial with "their identity and the mission in which they were lost" (258). No briefing, dialogue, or HQ beat is said to react to a death, a survivor, or a veteran.
- Editable callsigns (248) plus pre-generated voice lines (535, 627) mean authored dialogue probably can't address operators by name. The doc doesn't say how the story refers to the squad at all.
- On a successful extraction, any Downed operator "is lost" (308). If the whole squad is Downed, "the mission fails. The operation remains available for another attempt" (312), and the doc never says those operators are lost. Read literally, losing everyone costs nothing permanent, while losing one costs a person. That reverses the stakes the pillar depends on.
- The "explicit commit to leaving an operator behind" (117, 308) is the game's most charged moral moment. The doc treats it only as a procedure, with no fictional framing (capture? death? does the Directorate use the prisoner?), even though the campaign opens with the player escaping Directorate captivity.

**Why it matters.** Attachment is a core pillar, but nothing in the authored narrative gives it a response. Attachment that rests only on player projection, with failure rules that can erase the stakes, won't deliver "people the player wants to bring home."

## 5. Mutually exclusive choices are framed as loadout picks while hiding moral stakes, and the road not taken disappears — MINOR

**Problem.** The choice table (355–360) includes real ethical dilemmas, but the doc describes them as "a useful outcome in the context of the resistance's current needs" (353). Their effects are described only through equipment: "These choices influence preparation" (362).

**Evidence.**
- "Open the Cages or Take the Assembly Line — Rescue civilians and skilled workers, or secure local weapons production" (357). Choosing production means leaving imprisoned civilians in Directorate hands. Given Pillar 4 ("Rescue brings people into the resistance," 72) and a campaign that starts with the player escaping a cell, this choice touches the game's central theme. Yet it has no stated narrative consequence and no acknowledgment.
- "Bring Them Home or Chain of Command" (360) reuses the tagline's language for an optional branch that the player can reject.
- "Completing one advances the campaign and closes the other opportunity" (353). Nothing records or reflects what happened to the people or place the player didn't choose.

**Why it matters.** Either commit to these as story choices (acknowledge the cost in later briefings or HQ) or rename and reframe them so they don't promise moral weight the structure won't deliver. As written, the theme is raised and then flattened into an equipment pick.

## Round 2 — Cross-examination

### CONFLICTS

**C1. Business Analyst #2 and Feasibility Lead #5 (cut the character creator down to callsigns, presets and the memorial).** I accept the cost argument, but not the claim that "callsigns, a small set of presets, mission history, and the memorial would deliver most of the attachment." That only holds if something else carries the attachment the creator was carrying. Right now nothing does. Line 440 ("Operator identities remain open to the player's interpretation") explicitly hands character entirely to player projection, and the appearance editor (248) is the main tool the document gives the player for that projection. Take it away without adding authored acknowledgment and the "squad with an identity" pillar has neither player-made nor designer-made content behind it. My counter-proposal is that the cut be **paired**: drop the creator, but spend a small amount of the saved budget on cheap narrative carriers. Examples: a one-line generated background per recruit (for instance, which Directorate logistics post they deserted, which also pays off Finding 1), briefing and HQ lines that react to a death, a return from the wounded, or a veteran milestone, and memorial text that records how the operator was lost, not only where. Text costs far less than fitting gear across body types. The analysts are right on cost and wrong to assume attachment survives the cut on its own.

**C2. Systems Designer #1 (proposed fix: "a failed operation closes or escalates").** All four of us (Systems #1, Psychologist #1, QA #1, my #4) found the wipe-beats-loss inversion, so the diagnosis isn't in dispute. One of the Systems fixes is, though. "Closes" can't apply to the spine of the campaign. Sever the Ring, Break the Bastion and Their Last Order are single-path story beats (344–347). Closing them on failure either breaks the campaign or forces fallback content that the scope reviewers already say can't be afforded. Systems' *other* option, "Downed operators are captured or lost on a wipe," is the narratively correct one, and I would argue for it specifically. The campaign opens with a prisoner escaping Directorate custody (214) and contains a choice literally called Open the Cages (357). Capture on a wipe turns the exploit into story: the player has to pay for the failure, and the game gets a fiction for "left behind" that it currently lacks (my #4). Systems should keep that option and drop "closes."

**C3. Player Psychologist #5 rates the undefined opening prisoner MINOR. I hold MAJOR (my #2).** The Psychologist frames it as late attachment: will the first character the player protects carry forward? That is real, but it's the smaller half of the problem. Line 422 says "the player becomes one of its skilled operators." If that prisoner is the player-character and can die permanently under 258/308, the story loses its protagonist halfway through the campaign with no defined consequence. If the prisoner is not the player, line 422 is wrong and the briefing voice (436, 581) has nobody to address. This isn't a first-hour retention issue. It decides who the story is about, and it has to be settled before any voiceover script is written.

**C4. Player Psychologist #4 (kit lockouts should be previewed or trialled).** I agree players need to know what they're giving up. But if the choice screen previews only kits ("Medic vs Captain"), it finishes the flattening I flagged in #5: the medical team becomes a kit card. The preview should lead with the people and place left behind, then show the kit consequence. Putting the kit first solves the regret problem by giving up the theme. Putting the people first solves both.

### CONNECTIONS

**X1. The tagline-named branch decides whether the player can bring people home (my #5 + Systems #4 + Psychologist #4).** Systems and the Psychologist show that "Bring Them Home or Chain of Command" (360) locks the Medic. The Medic is the only stated source of ranged Rescue (206, 388), and the example squad depends on it (107). Combined with my lens, this is worse than a balance gap. The branch is named after the game's tagline ("bring your operators home," 7), and choosing *against* it makes it mechanically harder for the rest of the campaign to bring operators home. Whether intended or not, that is a strong thematic structure: the player trades their own squad's safety net for command capability. But the document presents it as "a useful outcome in the context of the resistance's current needs" (353) and never acknowledges what's at stake. Either embrace it (make the trade explicit and let later briefings remember it) or rename the branch so it doesn't borrow the tagline.

**X2. Rescued people are hollow on both axes (my #3/#5 + Adversarial QA #5).** QA found that escorted and rescued NPCs have no damage, death or failure rules (298, 312). My lens found they have no names, no lines and no story role. Rescue is reduced to a kit unlock (360, and 396: "A rescued team opens a related support capability"). Put together, Pillar 4 ("Rescue brings people into the resistance," 72) has nothing behind it either mechanically or in the fiction. In Open the Cages, the civilians can't be lost in play and aren't remembered in story. Rescue only means something if failure is possible and the rescued are seen again. Right now neither is true.

**X3. The unused insider premise is the missing differentiator (my #1 + Business Analyst #4).** The Business Analyst says the game reads as the XCOM 2 template (elite squad, resistance HQ, war table, memorial) and gives no reason to choose it over that. The document already contains the answer and doesn't use it: "Nova's resistance grows from the Directorate's own logistics organization" (422). A resistance made of former occupation logisticians who attack the supply system they built is a premise XCOM doesn't have. It would also give the generated side missions (326–328: supplies, intelligence, caches, stockpiles) a narrative reason for their objectives. Those are exactly the jobs an insider network would know how to do. So the positioning gap and the narrative gap have the same fix.

**X4. The pipeline assumes characters the design never writes (my #2/#3 + Business Analyst #5).** The Business Analyst notes that Sections 7–8 read as a separate document. My lens adds a concrete break between the two halves. The Voiceover Agent contract (581) requires a "speaker identity" per line, and pre-generated voice (535, 627) can't speak player-edited callsigns (248). Yet Sections 1–6 name no speaker: no handler, no Nova leader, no Directorate voice. The pipeline describes content that the design side hasn't authored. In the eight-week scope (Feasibility #1, Business #1), defining two or three named voices costs almost nothing and unblocks an entire agent role.

**X5. The fiction of "Downed" would settle QA's open rules (my #4 + Adversarial QA #2 + Psychologist #2).** QA asks whether Downed operators can be finished off, and whether enemies target them. The Psychologist shows that Downed operators are abandoned automatically with no confirmation (308). Both stem from the omission I flagged: the document never says what being "lost" *is* in the fiction. If lost means captured, enemies would plausibly secure Downed operators rather than execute them, abandonment needs a deliberate, acknowledged confirmation (which is what the Psychologist wants), and capture creates hooks for later rescue. If lost means death, executing Downed operators is consistent and the memorial makes sense. Deciding the fiction first gives designers a basis for these rules instead of patching them one at a time. QA's line-117-vs-308 contradiction also has a narrative tiebreaker. Line 117 (Downed operators *must* be revived or explicitly left behind) expresses the stated design intent. Line 308's silent automatic loss of a Downed operator is the version that betrays it.

### REVISIONS

- **#5 upgraded MINOR → MAJOR.** In Round 1 I treated the choice pairs as thematic flattening. X1 shows that the one branch named after the tagline controls the main mechanical tool for keeping operators alive, and X2 shows the rescue side of these branches has no stakes in play either. The mismatch between what these choices claim and what they deliver is structural, not cosmetic.
- **#4 narrowed, severity unchanged (MAJOR).** The wipe-inversion part of #4 is now covered by three other reviewers, two of whom rate it BLOCKING. I defer to them on severity for the systems exploit and won't double-count it. One calibration for the board: line 380 says "A mission's conclusion resolves its rewards and losses," which *could* be read to cover failed missions too. So the inversion is better described as an unresolved ambiguity between 312 and 380 than as a stated rule. It still needs fixing, but the document is silent rather than explicitly wrong. My own finding narrows to what's left from my lens: the authored narrative never acknowledges loss, and "left behind" has no fiction (X5).
- **#1 holds at MAJOR, priority raised.** Business #4 makes the unused insider premise matter beyond story craft (X3). I'm not raising it to BLOCKING, because the game is playable without it, but I would now rank it as my top recommendation. It is the cheapest fix with the widest effect.
- **#3 holds at MAJOR, with a scope-aware fix.** Given Feasibility #1 and Business #1, I don't ask for a cast. The minimum is a named handler voice, a named Directorate commander introduced before Their Last Order, and the medical team/field commander given one line each when rescued. That is enough to make the climax and the rescue branches land, and it supplies the speaker identities the voiceover pipeline already assumes (X4).
